\begin{abstract}
Learning to simulating human users could advance the training of human-centered agents, the evaluation of interactive systems, and research in the social sciences.  Existing approaches generally do so through training an LLM to match a single ground-truth response, by maximizing the log-probability or using a similarity-based reward. We instead propose a Turing test-based approach for learning user simulator models, where a model is be trained via RL to produce responses indistinguishable from what the user could have said, following the criterion of the Turing test. Our discriminative Turing reward uses an LM judge to score how indistinguishable a generated response is from the real user's given the user's history. Across two domains that differ in structure---multi-turn chat and Reddit forum discussion---we find that the Turing reward consistently outperforms baseline methods on automatic and human evaluation metrics. Our study suggests that optimizing for indistinguishability, rather than response matching, is an  effective route to learning LM-based user simulators.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The currently dominant use cases of LLMs involves having them act as helpful assistants to human users. However, a growing range of applications would benefit  from their playing  the opposite role, i.e.,  not the assistant, but the user.
% Such user simulators could serve as testbeds for conversational agents~\citep{naous2025flipping}, training environments for interactive systems~\citep{abdulhai2025consistently, mehri2025goal}, and proxies for studying human responses at scale~\citep{park2023generative, aher2023simulating, lu2025multiturn}.
Such user simulators could serve as fundamental building blocks for social world models in AI agents \cite{rabinowitz2018machine, collins2024building}, training environments and testbeds for interactive systems~\citep{abdulhai2025consistently, mehri2025goal}, and proxies for studying human responses at scale~\citep{park2023generative, aher2023simulating, lu2025multiturn}.\footnote{User simulators could also have harmful applications, such as in enabling fraud and distribution of misinformation at scale. See our discussion in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.}

To simulate a person is to model what makes them \textit{them}. This is fundamentally difficult: users are not interchangeable, and what distinguishes one person from another resists easy categorization. Two people with identical demographics can hold sharply different opinions~\citep{hwang2023aligning, santurkar2023opinions}, and individual preferences cannot be recovered from group-level labels alone~\citep{jiang2024indievalue, kirk2024prism}. Recent work has begun addressing this challenge through purpose-built user language models~\citep{naous2025flipping}, latent user-state alignment~\citep{wu2026humanlm}, log-probability maximization with latent reasoning~\citep{gandhi2026dialogue}, and persona induction from interaction history~\citep{ryan2025synthesizeme}. These approaches share a common assumption: the training signal is derived from matching a specific ground-truth response, whether by scoring similarity against it with an LLM judge or by directly maximizing its log-probability. Such strategies overlook a basic property of human communication---the same person in the same context could say many different things, all authentically their own. An ideal user simulator model should therefore go beyond replicating the ground-truth response, and instead produce responses indistinguishable from what the user {could have said}.

% such strategies which encourage models to replicate the ground-truth response is not goal of user simulation, where we are interested in not necessarily  faithful simulator is not to merely reproduce what the user happened to say, but to produce responses indistinguishable from what the user could have said.

% This overlooks a basic property of human communication, where the same person in the same context could say many different things, all authentically their own.



The classic Turing test \citep{turing1950computing} operationalizes human-likeness through indistinguishability:  a machine passes if an evaluator cannot tell its responses from a person's. This is precisely the criterion we want from a user simulator, and it translates directly into a training signal. In this paper, we therefore train LLM user simulators via reinforcement learning on a \emph{discriminative Turing reward}, wherein an LLM judge assigns a high score when a generated response is indistinguishable from the real user's, conditioned on the user's history, which includes the current session history and the user's behaviors in previous sessions.

We evaluate our Turing reward-trained LLMs across two settings that differ substantially in structure, in particular multi-turn dialogue~\citep{kirk2024prism} and Reddit forum discussions~\citep{chang2020convokit}. We compare against baseline training signals that have been previously explored in the literature: a response-similarity reward adapted from HumanLM~\citep{wu2026humanlm}, and log-probability maximization with latent chain-of-thought~\citep{gandhi2026dialogue}. We find that the Turing reward consistently outperforms both alternatives across both automatic and human evaluation metrics. \subsection{Discriminative Turing Reward}\label{sec:training}
We use a discriminative Turing reward as the training signal. Concretely, an LLM judge is shown the user's  history $(x, h)$ alongside two responses to the same context: one written by the real user from the ground-truth distribution $y_{\star} \sim p_\star(\cdot | x,h)$ and one sampled from the model $y \sim p_\theta( \cdot | x,h, \rho)$. The judge rates on a $\{1, 2, \dots, 7\}$ Likert scale which response was written by the human, considering consistency with the user's prior writing, stylistic fit, and naturalness. A score of $1$ means that the judge deems th human-generated response $y_{\star}$ to be more likely to be written by the human, while a score of $7$ means the judge deems the model-generated response $y$ is more likely to be written by the human.\footnote{We randomize the ordering of the responses given to the judge to mitigate against ordering bias.} Rather than optimizing for content overlap with a specific ground-truth response, this signal trains the model toward the broader quality of being indistinguishable from the target user. The full judge prompt is given in Appendix~\ref{app:turing-prompt}.

Letting $s({y, y_\star}) \in \{1, \dots, 7\}$ be the score assigned by the LLM judge, we cap and  normalize the score to $[0,1]$ the Turing reward 
\begin{align*}
r_{\text{turing}} (y, y_\star) =  \frac{\min\{s(y, y_{\star}), 4\} -1}{6}.
\end{align*}
The reward cap on $4$ mitigates against reward hacking: since we would like to produce responses that are human-like, a model that produces responses that are ``more human'' (as deemed by the judge) than actual human responses would be undesirable and would be indicative of reward hacking.\footnote{We indeed found this type of reward hacking to occur in practice in preliminary experiments.}

The final RL objective is then given by
\begin{align*}
    \max_\theta \, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p_\theta(\cdot | x, h, \rho), y_\star \sim p_\star(\cdot | x, h)}\left[ r_{\text{turing}} (y, y_\star) \right],
\end{align*}

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Datasets}
We evaluate our approach on two domains: Reddit and multi-turn chat. Both domains contain users and their interactions. We reserve a fixed block of prior interactions as behavior history $h$, and use the remaining interactions as prediction targets. We split users into training and evaluation sets. Full preprocessing details and split statistics are given in Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-stats}.

\paragraph{(Reddit) Online forum discussion: ConvoKit.}
The ConvoKit subreddit corpus~\citep{chang2020convokit} contains  discussions on Reddit. We select 14 subreddits, selecting users with at least 8 threads (1,282 users), and hold out \texttt{r/tifu} and \texttt{r/worldnews} for evaluation (102 users, 267 examples), with no user overlap with training. Each target thread yields one example: the user's last comment, and the earlier comments  as context.

\paragraph{(Chat) Multi-turn chat: PRISM.}
The PRISM Alignment Dataset~\citep{kirk2024prism} contains multi-turn conversations between humans and LLM assistants, spanning 1,500 participants from 75 countries. We select users with at least 6 conversations (1,288 users) and hold out 128 users (880 examples) for evaluation. Each user turn in a target conversation yields one training example.


\subsection{Training}
\paragraph{SFT warm start.}
Before RL, we warm-start the policy with SFT on ground-truth user responses augmented with chain-of-thought reasoning traces. For each training example, we use the untuned instruct model to generate a reasoning trace given the context and ground-truth response, explaining what could have led the user to write that response. The SFT target pairs the trace with the ground truth: \texttt{<reasoning>}$t$\texttt{</reasoning> [HUMAN]:}~$y$. The model is trained with LoRA using completion-only loss. At inference time, the model generates both the trace and the response autoregressively. See Appendix~\ref{app:thinking-trace-prompt} for the trace elicitation prompt.

\paragraph{RL training.}
We optimize the Turing discriminative judge signal with GRPO~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. We sample 4 candidate responses per training example and computes advantages via within-group normalization. All models are initialized from the SFT checkpoint discussed above and trained with LoRA ($r{=}64$, $\alpha{=}32$) for 3 epochs. GRPO training uses a dataset disjoint from the SFT training set.
We  additionally apply \emph{length penalty term} that penalize responses falling outside a tolerance band around the ground-truth length. We use Qwen3.5-397B-A17B~\citep{qwenteam2026qwen35omni} as the reward model.  Full training hyperparameters are given in Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\subsubsection{LLM-as-Judge Evaluation}
We automatically evaluate generated responses along three dimensions, each operationalized via an LLM judge. We use Claude Sonnet 4.6~\citep{anthropic2026claudesonnet46} for all evaluations.

\paragraph{Turing distinguishability.}
 A judge is presented with the user's behavioral history, the interaction context, and two responses: the ground-truth human response and a model-generated candidate, with position randomized. The judge rates which response was written by the real user on a 1--7 scale across three criteria (immediate target, human goal, and communication style). To control for position bias, we evaluate each pair in both orderings and average the  scores. Higher scores indicate the generated response was harder to distinguish from the real one. This metric mirrors our training signal but makes use of a more powerful judge than is used for training. See Appendix~\ref{app:turing-prompt} for the full judge prompt. 

\paragraph{Response similarity to ground truth.} We also measure similarity between the generated and ground truth responses, following \citet{wu2026humanlm}.  An LLM judge scores how well a generated response captures the overall content of the ground-truth human response. The judge extracts key points from the ground truth, then scores semantic similarity and information completeness, penalizing extraneous content, wrong perspective, and source copying. This yields a single score in $[0, 1]$ measuring whether the model said roughly what the real user said. See Appendix~\ref{app:sim-prompt} for the full judge prompt.

\paragraph{Context and user specificity.}
We finally evaluate whether a generated response is specifically grounded in the current interaction and compatible with the target user, rather than being a generic but plausible reply, which is a common failure of neural conversational models \citep{li2016diversity}. The judge scores two dimensions: \emph{context specificity} (how tightly the response engages with the exact local interaction) and \emph{user evidence compatibility} (how compatible the response is with the target user's observed behavior without unnaturally exposing or inventing evidence), each weighted $0.5$. This yields a single score in $[0, 1]$. For better calibration, we batch judge the specificity scores of multiple responses in one judge call. See Appendix~\ref{app:specificity-prompt} for the full judge prompt.

\subsubsection{Human Evaluation}

In addition to LLM-as-judge evaluation, we also recruit crowd workers via Prolific for a forced-choice Turing test: given a target user's history and two candidate responses (one real, one model-generated) in randomized order, annotators select which was written by the real user. We evaluate three models: LLM trained with just supervised finetuning (SFT-Init), RL-training with the similarity reward (GRPO-Sim), and RL-training with our Turing reward (GRPO-Turing). We target 50 users per dataset, collecting ${\sim}300$ binary judgments per condition (6 judgments for each target user) after filtering for comprehension. We report the model win rate (WR), where WR ${>} 0.5$ means the model fools human annotators above chance. Full annotation details are given in Appendix~\ref{app:human-eval}.

Figure~\ref{fig:turing_dot} shows Turing judge scores across both domains. GRPO-Turing substantially outperforms all other models, including GRPO-Sim and SFT-Init, in both Reddit and Chat. The gap is especially large on Chat, where GRPO-Turing exceeds the next-best model by a wide margin. 

Notably, user simulation remains a difficult task overall. Even GPT-5, a much larger model, scores only slightly above SFT-Init. Qualitative inspection reveals that GPT-5 tends to produce verbose, overly hedged responses that read as assistant-like rather than human-like (see Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative_examples} in the appendix). Training with the similarity reward does not significantly improve the Turing score over the SFT-Init checkpoint, suggesting that matching the content of the ground-truth response does not translate to human-like indistinguishability.

\paragraph{Response similarity to ground-truth.}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\label{fig:sim_dot}

Figure~\ref{fig:sim_dot} reports similarity to the ground-truth response. GPT-5 achieves notably high similarity on Reddit, likely because its verbose outputs happen to cover more of the ground-truth key points, even though this verbosity hurts its Turing score. Among the trained models, GRPO-Sim and GRPO-Turing perform comparably, both improving over the SFT model. This confirms that training with the Turing reward does not sacrifice content alignment: GRPO-Turing produces responses that are both hard to distinguish from the real user and similar in content to what the user actually said.

\paragraph{Response specificity.}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/sonnet46_specificity_dot.pdf}
\caption{Response specificity scores across Reddit (top) and Chat (bottom). The dashed line and shaded band show the Qwen3-8B baseline mean and 95\% CI.}

Beyond matching content, we measure whether generated responses are specifically grounded in the interaction context and compatible with the target user, rather than being generic but plausible replies. Figure~\ref{fig:specificity} shows that GRPO-Turing and GRPO-Sim produce the most contextually specific responses in both domains, substantially outperforming the base model and SFT-Init. On Reddit, the ground truth (GT) scores highest, confirming that real users produce highly specific responses. On Chat, however, GT specificity is comparable to the base model, reflecting that real users in open-ended dialogue often pivot to new topics rather than tightly engaging with prior context (see Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative_examples}).

\subsection{Human Evaluation}
Table~\ref{tab:human_eval_winrate} reports mean win rates over ground-truth. The Turing reward is the only training signal that consistently improves win rate above chance in both domains, confirming that optimizing for indistinguishability transfers to real human judges. The ranking across models closely mirrors the automatic Turing scores in Figure~\ref{fig:turing_dot}, validating the LLM judge as a reasonable proxy for human distinguishability. Notably, SFT-Init is reliably detected on Reddit (WR $= .42$), suggesting that imitation learning alone produces surface-level patterns that humans can identify.

We test whether each condition's win rate differs from chance using one-sided tests. On Chat, GRPO-Turing approaches significance in a binomial test of whether WR ${>} 0.5$ over all 300 pooled judgments at $p = .053$. On Reddit, SFT-Init falls significantly \emph{below} chance at $p < .05$, meaning human annotators can reliably identify its outputs as machine-generated. Because the same 50 target users are shared across all conditions within each dataset, we can also run paired comparisons. Paired $t$-tests show that GRPO-Turing significantly outperforms SFT-Init on Reddit at $p = .024$. The same trend holds on Chat at $p = .133$, though it does not reach significance. The difference between GRPO-Turing and GRPO-Sim is positive in both domains but does not reach significance, which we attribute to the relatively small sample size of ${\sim}30$ annotators per condition.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
A recurring finding across our experiments is that content matching and human-likeness come apart. While similarity reward improves how well a response covers the ground-truth content, it does not make the response harder to distinguish from the real user. The Turing reward, in contrast, improves indistinguishability without lowering similarity. This suggests that what makes a response recognizably machine-generated lies largely in surface form, and that a discriminative signal is better suited than a matching signal for user simulation.

User simulators that are faithful to specific people also raise misuse concerns. A model that produces responses indistinguishable from a target user, conditioned on that user's history, is well suited to impersonation, and could be used to fabricate messages attributed to real people or to manufacture the appearance of grassroots opinion. We train and evaluate only on public or consented research data, and our interest is in modeling users in aggregate rather than reproducing identifiable individuals. We also note that the discriminator we train against is itself a starting point for detection: the same signal that exposes machine-generated tells during training could be used to flag synthetic responses at deployment.
\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{LM-based user simulation.}
LLMs have been used to evaluate dialogue systems~\citep{davidson2023user, sekulic2024simulating}, train conversational agents via self-play~\citep{shah2018bootstrapping, abdulhai2025consistently}, and replicate human subject studies~\citep{aher2023simulating, park2023generative}. \citet{naous2025flipping} show that assistant-tuned LLMs are structurally misaligned with the user role, while \citet{mehri2025goal} address goal drift in task-oriented simulation through explicit state tracking. These works establish that user simulation benefits from purpose-built training but model generic users rather than specific individuals.

\paragraph{Training objectives for human simulation.}
Beyond standard supervised fine-tuning~\citep{wolf2019transfertransfo}, \citet{wu2026humanlm} optimize for similarity along psychologically grounded dimensions such as stance and emotion, \citet{abdulhai2025consistently} use LLM-judged consistency scores as PPO rewards, and \citet{gandhi2026dialogue} show that log-probability maximization with latent chain-of-thought outperforms judge-based rewards in generic dialogue prediction. Our discriminative Turing reward takes a different approach: rather than matching content or maximizing likelihood, it trains the model to be indistinguishable from the real user. We show this signal generalizes across evaluation metrics and interaction formats.

\paragraph{Individual user representation.}
Persona-conditioned generation was introduced by \citet{zhang2018personalizing} and extended with pretrained models by \citet{wolf2019transfertransfo}. Subsequent work enriches user representations: \citet{hwang2023aligning} show that retrieving relevant past opinions outperforms demographic prompting; \citet{ryan2025synthesizeme} induce synthetic personas from behavioral traces for personalized reward modeling; and \citet{jiang2024indievalue} demonstrate that individual values cannot be approximated by group-level categories. We combine raw history with an induced persona, providing both behavioral evidence and a compressed trait summary.

\paragraph{Human behavior prediction} Predicting human behaviors, such as people's decisions, actions, and utterances, is a cornerstone task cognitive and social sciences.

\section{Dataset Details}
\label{app:dataset-stats}

Each training example is a tuple $(u, x, y)$ where the user representation $u = (h, \rho)$ is fixed per user and disjoint from both the current context $x$ and the ground-truth response $y$. The behavioral history $h$ is selected once per user from a deterministic seed; the persona $\rho$ is induced from $h$ alone. This separation guarantees that neither $h$ nor $\rho$ leaks information about the target.

\paragraph{PRISM.}
We retain PRISM participants with at least six conversations, yielding 1,288 qualified users. We randomly hold out 10\% of qualified users with a fixed seed, giving 128 held-out test users and 880 test examples. The remaining 1,160 users are split with the same seed into disjoint GRPO and SFT pools using a 60/40 user ratio: 696 users for GRPO and 464 users for SFT. Per user, 2--4 conversations are reserved as $h$; the rest serve as targets. Because PRISM dialogues are linear, each user turn in a target conversation is one example: $x$ is all preceding turns and $y$ is the user's utterance at that turn. GRPO train and validation examples are split within each GRPO user using a deterministic tail-split policy: for a user with $n$ target rows, $\lfloor 0.1n \rfloor$ rows, with a minimum of one when $n>1$, are assigned to validation. Before applying this tail split, target rows are sorted by conversation id and target turn index, so validation rows come from later target turns and training does not use a later target from the same conversation than validation.

\paragraph{ConvoKit.}
We reconstruct Reddit threads from comment identifiers and parent pointers. Deleted, removed, and non-user comments cannot serve as targets or enter $h$, but non-root unusable comments may be kept as structural anchors so that descendant replies stay connected to the ancestor chain. We reserve \texttt{r/tifu} and \texttt{r/worldnews} as held-out test subreddits and remove their users from both SFT and GRPO training. The remaining users are split deterministically into disjoint SFT and GRPO pools using a 40/60 ratio. GRPO train and validation examples are then split within each GRPO user using the same deterministic tail-split policy as the preprocessing pipeline: for a user with $n$ target rows, $\lfloor 0.3n \rfloor$ rows, with a minimum of one when $n>1$, are assigned to validation and the earlier rows to training. This gives every GRPO training user at least one validation row; users with only one eligible row are dropped from GRPO train/validation. Per user, 2--6 threads are reserved as $h$. Each target thread yields one example: $y$ is the user's final comment and $x$ is the original post plus the ancestor reply chain leading to it, avoiding highly correlated examples from the same thread.

\subsection{Turing Reward Shaping}
\label{app:reward-shaping}

The Turing reward applies two shaping mechanisms not used by the similarity or log-probability rewards.

\paragraph{Length deadband.}
Let $\ell$ be the generated response length and $\ell_{\mathrm{gt}}$ the ground-truth length. Responses within the deadband $[\ell_{\min} \cdot \ell_{\mathrm{gt}},\; \ell_{\max} \cdot \ell_{\mathrm{gt}}]$ incur no penalty. Outside the band, the penalty is proportional to the relative violation with asymmetric lambdas (stronger for short responses), and capped:
\begin{align}
p_{\mathrm{short}} &= \lambda_{\mathrm{short}} \cdot \max\!\bigl(\ell_{\min} - \ell/\ell_{\mathrm{gt}},\; 0\bigr) / \ell_{\min} \nonumber\\
p_{\mathrm{long}} &= \lambda_{\mathrm{long}} \cdot \max\!\bigl(\ell/\ell_{\mathrm{gt}} - \ell_{\max},\; 0\bigr) / \ell_{\max} \nonumber\\
p_{\mathrm{len}} &= \min(p_{\mathrm{short}} + p_{\mathrm{long}},\; p_{\mathrm{cap}})
\end{align}

\paragraph{Score clipping.}
The raw Turing judge score (1--7) is clipped to a maximum of $s_{\mathrm{clip}}$ before canonicalization, reducing the influence of extreme ratings.

\section{Human Evaluation Details}
\label{app:human-eval}

We conduct a forced-choice Turing test on Prolific. Each participant views a target user's posting history and then judges 10 pairs of responses: one ground-truth response and one model-generated response, presented in randomized order. The participant selects which response they believe was written by the real user.

\paragraph{Design.}
We evaluate three training conditions---SFT-Init, GRPO-Sim, and GRPO-Turing---on two datasets (Reddit / ConvoKit and Chat / PRISM). For each dataset we select 50 target users, split into 5 groups of 10. Each participant is assigned to one (dataset, condition, group) cell and evaluates the 10 targets in that group. We recruit 6 annotators per cell, yielding 30 participants and 300 binary judgments per condition.

\paragraph{Comprehension filtering.}
Each trial includes comprehension checks that test whether the participant read the target user's history (e.g., identifying the user's most-discussed topic or their most recent interlocutor). We compute each participant's comprehension accuracy across all check questions and exclude participants scoring below 75\%. One participant was excluded under this criterion (PRISM/Turing, group~3; accuracy ${=} 0.65$).

\paragraph{Balancing.}
After excluding incomplete submissions (participants with fewer than 10 judgments) and those failing the comprehension filter, we retain the first 6 completions per (dataset, condition, group) cell, ordered by submission time. This ensures exactly 30 participants and 300 votes per condition. Three cells required trimming of one excess participant each (PRISM/Sim group~2, PRISM/Turing groups~1 and~4).

\paragraph{Metric.}
We report the model win rate (WR): the fraction of judgments in which the annotator chose the model-generated response over ground truth. WR is computed per target user (averaging over the ${\sim}6$ annotators for that target) and then averaged across the 50 targets. The 95\% confidence interval is $\text{mean} \pm 1.96 \times \text{SEM}$, where SEM is the standard error of the 50 per-target win rates.

\subsection{Inference Prompt}
\label{app:inference-prompt}

At inference time, the model receives a system message containing the task instruction and (optionally) the user's persona, followed by a user message with the behavioral history and current context. The task instruction asks the model to predict \texttt{[HUMAN]}'s next message, matching writing intentions, style, vocabulary, and tone. Before outputting the final answer, the model is instructed to reason step by step, producing output in the format: \texttt{<reasoning>...</reasoning> [HUMAN]:} response.
