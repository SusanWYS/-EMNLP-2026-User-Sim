\section{Introduction}
The currently dominant use cases of LLMs involves having them act as helpful assistants to human users. However, a growing range of applications would benefit  from their playing  the opposite role, i.e.,  not the assistant, but the user.
% Such user simulators could serve as testbeds for conversational agents~\citep{naous2025flipping}, training environments for interactive systems~\citep{abdulhai2025consistently, mehri2025goal}, and proxies for studying human responses at scale~\citep{park2023generative, aher2023simulating, lu2025multiturn}.
Such user simulators could serve as fundamental building blocks for social world models in AI agents \cite{rabinowitz2018machine, collins2024building}, training environments and testbeds for interactive systems~\citep{abdulhai2025consistently, mehri2025goal}, and proxies for studying human responses at scale~\citep{park2023generative, aher2023simulating, lu2025multiturn}.\footnote{User simulators could also have harmful applications, such as in fraud and distribution of misinformation at scale. See our discussion in \S.}

To simulate a person is to model what makes them \textit{them}. This is fundamentally difficult: users are not interchangeable, and what distinguishes one person from another resists easy categorization. Two people with identical demographics can hold sharply different opinions~\citep{hwang2023aligning, santurkar2023opinions}, and individual preferences cannot be recovered from group-level labels alone~\citep{jiang2024indievalue, kirk2024prism}. Recent work has begun addressing this challenge through purpose-built user language models~\citep{naous2025flipping}, latent user-state alignment~\citep{wu2026humanlm}, log-probability maximization with latent reasoning~\citep{gandhi2026dialogue}, and persona induction from interaction history~\citep{ryan2025synthesizeme}. These approaches share a common assumption: the training signal is derived from matching a specific ground-truth response, whether by scoring similarity against it with an LLM judge or by directly maximizing its log-probability. Such strategies overlook a basic property of human communication---the same person in the same context could say many different things, all authentically their own. An ideal user simulator model should therefore go beyond replicating the ground-truth response, and instead produce responses indistinguishable from what the user {could have said}.

% such strategies which encourage models to replicate the ground-truth response is not goal of user simulation, where we are interested in not necessarily  faithful simulator is not to merely reproduce what the user happened to say, but to produce responses indistinguishable from what the user could have said.

% This overlooks a basic property of human communication, where the same person in the same context could say many different things, all authentically their own.



The classic Turing test \citep{turing1950computing} operationalizes human-likeness through indistinguishability:  a machine passes if an evaluator cannot tell its responses from a person's. This is precisely the criterion we want from a user simulator, and it translates directly into a training signal. In this paper, we therefore train LLM user simulators via reinforcement learning on a \emph{discriminative Turing reward}, wherein an LLM judge assigns a high score when a generated response is indistinguishable from the real user's, conditioned on the user's history, which includes the current session history and the user's behaviors in previous sessions.

We evaluate our Turing reward-trained LLMs across two settings that differ substantially in structure, in particular multi-turn dialogue~\citep{kirk2024prism} and Reddit forum discussions~\citep{chang2020convokit}. We compare against baseline training signals that have been previously explored in the literature: a response-similarity reward adapted from HumanLM~\citep{wu2026humanlm}, and log-probability maximization with latent chain-of-thought~\citep{gandhi2026dialogue}. We find that the Turing reward consistently outperforms both alternatives across both automatic and human evaluation metrics. \subsection{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

We study user simulation in an interactive setting, where we are given the current session context $x$ (i.e., prior interactions in the  current conversation session or thread) and {user history} $h$ (e.g., prior utterances or posts that are not part of the current session),  and must produce a response $y$ that could have been plausibly produced by the user.

\subsection{Latent User Personas}

We hypothesize that pairing raw behavioral history with an induced persona $\rho$ would yield a more stable user representation than just the raw history $h$ for producing $y$. We induce $\rho$ with via prompting an auxiliary language model to summarize stable traits from the user's  history  $h$, yielding the persona $\rho$. The persona is structured along five dimensions: \emph{values} (recurring priorities and themes), \emph{verbal features} (surface-form habits such as punctuation and formatting), \emph{expression style} (tone, formality, directness), \emph{length prior} (typical response length), and \emph{background} (self-disclosed roles, experiences, and domain familiarity). Each dimension is described by one to three concise sentences. The $\rho$ depends only on $h$ and never on the current target context $x$ or ground-truth response $y$. See the appendix for the prompt to the persona-induction LLM. We ablate on the use $\rho$ in our ablation study, where we find that $\rho$ is helpful on the chat domain.

For each user, we reserve a fixed block of $k$ prior interactions  once at dataset construction and reuse it as $h$ across every prediction target for that user. The same history block is the sole input to persona induction. We require $h$ to be disjoint from the current target context $x$ and ground-truth response $y$, so that the user representation is never contaminated by the example being predicted. The size $k$ is sampled per user from a small range, trading off the strength of the identity prior against the number of remaining targets.

\subsection{Discriminative Turing Reward}\label{sec:training}
We use a discriminative Turing reward as the training signal. Concretely, an LLM judge is shown the user's  history $(x, h)$ alongside two responses to the same context: one written by the real user from the ground-truth distribution $y_{\star} \sim p_\star(\cdot | x,h)$ and one sampled from the model $y \sim p_\theta( \cdot | x,h, \rho)$. The judge rates on a $\{1, 2, \dots, 7\}$ Likert scale which response was written by the human, considering consistency with the user's prior writing, stylistic fit, and naturalness. A score of $1$ means that the judge deems th human-generated response $y_{\star}$ to be more likely to be written by the human, while a score of $7$ means the judge deems the model-generated response $y$ is more likely to be written by the human.\footnote{We randomize the ordering of the responses given to the judge to mitigate against ordering bias.} Rather than optimizing for content overlap with a specific ground-truth response, this signal trains the model toward the broader quality of being indistinguishable from the target user. The full judge prompt is given in Appendix~\ref{app:turing-prompt}.

Letting $s({y, y_\star}) \in \{1, \dots, 7\}$ be the score assigned by the LLM judge, we cap and  normalize the score to $[0,1]$ the Turing reward 
\begin{align*}
r_{\text{turing}} (y, y_\star) =  \frac{\min\{s(y, y_{\star}), 4\} -1}{6}.
\end{align*}
The reward cap on $4$ mitigates against reward hacking: since we would like to produce responses that are human-like, a model that produces responses that are ``more human'' (as deemed by the judge) than actual human responses would be undesirable and would be indicative of reward hacking.\footnote{We indeed found this type of reward hacking to occur in practice in preliminary experiments.}

The final RL objective is then given by
\begin{align*}
    \max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p_\theta(\cdot | x, h, \rho), y_\star \sim p_\star(\cdot | x, h)}\left[ r_{\text{turing}} (y, y_\star) \right],
\end{align*}

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Datasets}

We evaluate across two interaction formats that differ in structure, register, and social context. In both datasets, users are partitioned into disjoint SFT, GRPO, and held-out test pools, and a fixed block of prior interactions per user is reserved as behavioral history $h$, disjoint from all prediction targets. Full preprocessing details and split statistics are given in Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-stats}.

\paragraph{(Chat) Multi-turn Chat: PRISM.}
The PRISM Alignment Dataset~\citep{kirk2024prism} contains multi-turn conversations between human participants and LLM assistants, spanning 1,500 participants from 75 countries. We qualify users with at least 6 conversations (1,288 users) and hold out 128 users (880 examples) for evaluation. Each user turn in a target conversation yields one training example.

\paragraph{(Reddit) Online Forum Discussion: ConvoKit.}
The ConvoKit subreddit corpus~\citep{chang2020convokit} contains threaded Reddit discussions. We select 14 subreddits, qualify users with at least 8 threads (1,282 users), and hold out \texttt{r/tifu} and \texttt{r/worldnews} for evaluation (102 users, 267 examples), with no user overlap with training. Each target thread yields one example: the user's last comment, with earlier comments retained as context.

\subsection{SFT Warm Start}

Before reinforcement learning, we warm-start the policy with supervised fine-tuning on ground-truth user responses augmented with chain-of-thought reasoning traces. For each training example, we use the untrained instruct model to generate a reasoning trace given the user representation and context, explaining what could lead the user to write the ground-truth response without reproducing it. The SFT target pairs the trace with the ground truth: \texttt{<reasoning>}$t$\texttt{</reasoning> [HUMAN]:}~$y$. The model is trained with LoRA using completion-only loss. At inference time, the model generates both the trace and the response autoregressively. See Appendix~\ref{app:thinking-trace-prompt} for the trace elicitation prompt.

\subsection{GRPO Training}

We optimize each reward signal with Group Relative Policy Optimization~\citep[GRPO;][]{shao2024deepseekmath}, which samples $G$ candidate responses per training example and computes advantages via within-group normalization, eliminating the need for a separate value model. All models are initialized from a SFT checkpoint and trained with LoRA ($r{=}64$, $\alpha{=}32$) for 3 epochs. Full training hyperparameters are given in Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}.

For the Turing reward, we apply two additional reward-shaping mechanisms: a \emph{length deadband} that penalizes responses falling outside a tolerance band around the ground-truth length, and \emph{score clipping} that caps the raw judge score to mitigate outlier influence. Neither mechanism is applied to the similarity or log-probability rewards. \llq{do we have a justification why not apply length penalty to other rewards? might need to discuss baselines before. also might need to add this ablation later} The deadband parameters and clipping thresholds are given in Appendix~\ref{app:reward-shaping}.

\subsection{Evaluation}

We evaluate generated responses along three complementary dimensions, each operationalized via an LLM judge.

\paragraph{Turing distinguishability.}
A judge is presented with the user's behavioral history, the interaction context, and two responses: the ground-truth human response and a model-generated candidate, with position randomized. The judge rates which response was written by the real user on a 1--7 scale across three criteria (immediate target, human goal, and communication style) with four penalty checks for artifacts. To control for position bias, we evaluate each pair in both orderings and average the canonicalized scores. Higher scores indicate the generated response was harder to distinguish from the real one. See Appendix~\ref{app:turing-prompt} for the full judge prompt.

\paragraph{Response Similarity to Ground Truth.}
Following \citet{wu2026humanlm}, an LLM judge scores how well a generated response captures the overall content of the ground-truth human response. The judge extracts key points from the ground truth, then scores semantic similarity and information completeness, penalizing extraneous content, wrong perspective, and source copying. This yields a single score in $[0, 1]$ measuring whether the model said roughly what the real user said. See Appendix~\ref{app:sim-prompt} for the full judge prompt.

\paragraph{Context and User Specificity.}
We evaluate whether a generated response is specifically grounded in the current interaction and compatible with the target user, rather than being a generic but plausible reply. The judge scores two dimensions: \emph{context specificity} (how tightly the response engages with the exact local interaction) and \emph{user evidence compatibility} (how compatible the response is with the target user's observed behavior without unnaturally exposing or inventing evidence), each weighted $0.5$. This yields a single score in $[0, 1]$. See Appendix~\ref{app:specificity-prompt} for the full judge prompt.

\subsection{Baselines and Ablations}

Our experiments are organized as follows.

\paragraph{Training signal comparison.}
We fix the user representation to $u = (h, \rho)$ and compare four training conditions: (1)~GRPO with Turing reward (proposed), (2)~GRPO with response similarity reward~\citep{wu2026humanlm}, (3)~GRPO with log-probability and latent thinking~\citep{gandhi2026dialogue}, and (4)~supervised fine-tuning (SFT). We also report the performance of the untrained base model (Qwen3-8B) and GPT-5 (minimal reasoning effort) as zero-shot references.

\paragraph{User representation ablation.}
We fix the training signal to the Turing reward and compare three input conditions: $u = h$ (history only), $u = \rho$ (persona only), and $u = (h, \rho)$ (both). This isolates the contribution of each component.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{LLM-based user simulation.}
LLMs have been used to evaluate dialogue systems~\citep{davidson2023user, sekulic2024simulating}, train conversational agents via self-play~\citep{shah2018bootstrapping, abdulhai2025consistently}, and replicate human subject studies~\citep{aher2023simulating, park2023generative}. \citet{naous2025flipping} show that assistant-tuned LLMs are structurally misaligned with the user role, while \citet{mehri2025goal} address goal drift in task-oriented simulation through explicit state tracking. These works establish that user simulation benefits from purpose-built training but model generic users rather than specific individuals.

\paragraph{Representing individual users.}
Persona-conditioned generation was introduced by \citet{zhang2018personalizing} and extended with pretrained models by \citet{wolf2019transfertransfo}. Subsequent work enriches user representations: \citet{hwang2023aligning} show that retrieving relevant past opinions outperforms demographic prompting; \citet{ryan2025synthesizeme} induce synthetic personas from behavioral traces for personalized reward modeling; and \citet{jiang2024indievalue} demonstrate that individual values cannot be approximated by group-level categories. We combine raw history with an induced persona, providing both behavioral evidence and a compressed trait summary.

\paragraph{Training objectives for human simulation.}
Beyond standard supervised fine-tuning~\citep{wolf2019transfertransfo}, \citet{wu2026humanlm} optimize for similarity along psychologically grounded dimensions such as stance and emotion, \citet{abdulhai2025consistently} use LLM-judged consistency scores as PPO rewards, and \citet{gandhi2026dialogue} show that log-probability maximization with latent chain-of-thought outperforms judge-based rewards in generic dialogue prediction. Our discriminative Turing reward takes a different approach: rather than matching content or maximizing likelihood, it trains the model to be indistinguishable from the real user. We show this signal generalizes across evaluation metrics and interaction formats.

\subsubsection{Inference Prompt}
\label{app:inference-prompt}

At inference time, the model receives a system message containing the task instruction and (optionally) the user's persona, followed by a user message with the behavioral history and current context. The task instruction asks the model to predict \texttt{[HUMAN]}'s next message, matching writing intentions, style, vocabulary, and tone. Before outputting the final answer, the model is instructed to reason step by step, producing output in the format: \texttt{<reasoning>...</reasoning> [HUMAN]:} response.

\subsection{Judge Prompts}
\label{app:judge-prompts}

\subsubsection{Turing Distinguishability Judge}
\label{app:turing-prompt}

The Turing judge receives the user's behavioral history, the current interaction context, and two candidate responses (one real, one generated) with positions randomized. It scores each response on three criteria and four penalty dimensions, then converts the score gap to a 1--7 rating.

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Criteria (each scored 0--1):}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Immediate target}: Does the response address the correct part of the context, from \texttt{[HUMAN]}'s perspective, directed at the appropriate speaker?
\item \textbf{Human goal}: Given the immediate target, is the response's communicative goal plausible for \texttt{[HUMAN]} in this context?
\item \textbf{Communication style}: Does the wording, tone, length, grammar, punctuation, and level of detail match \texttt{[HUMAN]}'s past responses?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Penalty checks (each scored 0--1):}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Source copy}: Unnatural copying from user history (natural quoting/uptake exempt).
\item \textbf{Wrong target or role}: Speaking from the wrong role or addressing the wrong speaker.
\item \textbf{Unsupported adversarial reframing}: Attacking a claim the other speaker did not make.
\item \textbf{Assistant-like}: Chatbot-style formatting, template phrases, or artifact-like surface form.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Scoring:} $\text{base} = \text{target} + \text{goal} + \text{style}$; $\text{penalty} = \frac{\sum \text{penalties}}{4} \times 3$; $\text{final} = \max(0, \text{base} - \text{penalty})$. The score gap between Response B and Response A is mapped to a 1--7 Likert rating (1 = definitely A is human, 7 = definitely B is human).
\end{quote}

\subsubsection{Response Similarity Judge}
\label{app:sim-prompt}

The response similarity judge evaluates how well a generated response captures the content of the ground-truth human response. It operates in batched mode, scoring multiple generations against a single ground truth.

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Inputs:} User history, current context, ground-truth response, and one or more generated responses.

\textbf{Scores (each in $[0, 1]$):}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Semantic similarity}: How similar the generated response is to the ground truth in core meaning and information ($1.0$ = identical meaning; $0.0$ = completely different topic).
\item \textbf{Information completeness}: How completely the generated response preserves information from the ground truth ($1.0$ = all information preserved; $0.0$ = almost no information preserved).
\item \textbf{Overall score}: A strict final alignment score penalizing unsupported extra content, wrong perspective, source copying, sycophantic/generic agreement, and generic compromise responses that dilute specific positions.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Hard rules:} If the generated response speaks from the wrong perspective (e.g., as an assistant rather than as the user), all scores are set to $0.0$.
\end{quote}

\subsubsection{Response Specificity Judge}
\label{app:specificity-prompt}

The specificity judge evaluates whether a generated response is specifically grounded in the current interaction and compatible with the target user, rather than being a generic but plausible reply.

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Inputs:} Target user evidence, interaction context, and one candidate response.

\textbf{Dimensions (each scored 0--1):}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Context specificity} (weight $0.5$): How specifically the candidate engages with the exact local interaction. High scores require tight ties to the exact claim, question, event, or disagreement at issue. Low scores indicate broadly reusable responses.
\item \textbf{User evidence compatibility} (weight $0.5$): How compatible the candidate is with the target user's observed behavior without unnaturally exposing or inventing evidence. High scores require natural compatibility; low scores indicate contradiction or persona-caricature.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Caps:} If the response contradicts the context or uses the wrong speaker perspective, overall $\leq 0.20$. If it invents personal facts or unnaturally imports user-history details, overall $\leq 0.40$.

\textbf{Overall:} $0.5 \times \text{context\_specificity} + 0.5 \times \text{user\_evidence\_compatibility}$, subject to the caps above.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Turing Reward Shaping}
\label{app:reward-shaping}

The Turing reward applies two shaping mechanisms not used by the similarity or log-probability rewards.

\paragraph{Length deadband.}
Let $\ell$ be the generated response length and $\ell_{\mathrm{gt}}$ the ground-truth length. Responses within the deadband $[\ell_{\min} \cdot \ell_{\mathrm{gt}},\; \ell_{\max} \cdot \ell_{\mathrm{gt}}]$ incur no penalty. Outside the band, the penalty is proportional to the relative violation with asymmetric lambdas (stronger for short responses), and capped:
\begin{align}
p_{\mathrm{short}} &= \lambda_{\mathrm{short}} \cdot \max\!\bigl(\ell_{\min} - \ell/\ell_{\mathrm{gt}},\; 0\bigr) / \ell_{\min} \nonumber\\
p_{\mathrm{long}} &= \lambda_{\mathrm{long}} \cdot \max\!\bigl(\ell/\ell_{\mathrm{gt}} - \ell_{\max},\; 0\bigr) / \ell_{\max} \nonumber\\
p_{\mathrm{len}} &= \min(p_{\mathrm{short}} + p_{\mathrm{long}},\; p_{\mathrm{cap}})
\end{align}

\paragraph{Score clipping.}
The raw Turing judge score (1--7) is clipped to a maximum of $s_{\mathrm{clip}}$ before canonicalization, reducing the influence of extreme ratings.

\subsection{Dataset Preprocessing and Statistics}
\label{app:dataset-stats}

Each training example is a tuple $(u, x, y)$ where the user representation $u = (h, \rho)$ is fixed per user and disjoint from both the current context $x$ and the ground-truth response $y$. The behavioral history $h$ is selected once per user from a deterministic seed; the persona $\rho$ is induced from $h$ alone. This separation guarantees that neither $h$ nor $\rho$ leaks information about the target.

\paragraph{PRISM.}
We retain PRISM participants with at least six conversations, yielding 1,288 qualified users. We randomly hold out 10\% of qualified users with a fixed seed, giving 128 held-out test users and 880 test examples. The remaining 1,160 users are split with the same seed into disjoint GRPO and SFT pools using a 60/40 user ratio: 696 users for GRPO and 464 users for SFT. Per user, 2--4 conversations are reserved as $h$; the rest serve as targets. Because PRISM dialogues are linear, each user turn in a target conversation is one example: $x$ is all preceding turns and $y$ is the user's utterance at that turn. GRPO train and validation examples are split within each GRPO user using a deterministic tail-split policy: for a user with $n$ target rows, $\lfloor 0.1n \rfloor$ rows, with a minimum of one when $n>1$, are assigned to validation. Before applying this tail split, target rows are sorted by conversation id and target turn index, so validation rows come from later target turns and training does not use a later target from the same conversation than validation.

\paragraph{ConvoKit.}
We reconstruct Reddit threads from comment identifiers and parent pointers. Deleted, removed, and non-user comments cannot serve as targets or enter $h$, but non-root unusable comments may be kept as structural anchors so that descendant replies stay connected to the ancestor chain. We reserve \texttt{r/tifu} and \texttt{r/worldnews} as held-out test subreddits and remove their users from both SFT and GRPO training. The remaining users are split deterministically into disjoint SFT and GRPO pools using a 40/60 ratio. GRPO train and validation examples are then split within each GRPO user using the same deterministic tail-split policy as the preprocessing pipeline: for a user with $n$ target rows, $\lfloor 0.3n \rfloor$ rows, with a minimum of one when $n>1$, are assigned to validation and the earlier rows to training. This gives every GRPO training user at least one validation row; users with only one eligible row are dropped from GRPO train/validation. Per user, 2--6 threads are reserved as $h$. Each target thread yields one example: $y$ is the user's final comment and $x$ is the original post plus the ancestor reply chain leading to it, avoiding highly correlated examples from the same thread.

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{SFT/GRPO pool:} AmItheAsshole, AskMen, AskWomen, business, changemyview, Economics, Frugal, MaliciousCompliance, news, relationship\_advice, relationships, TrueReddit \\
\textbf{Held-out test:} tifu, worldnews
\end{quote}
